A quiet pager is a design decision

Most overnight alerts are not emergencies. They are decisions nobody made on a Tuesday afternoon, when it was cheap to make them.

By Nadia Rowe 󰃭 9 Nov 2026 󰅐 8 min read Operations On-call

The page that did not matter

A small team inherited a monitoring setup with 140 alert rules. Nobody could say who had written most of them. Each rule had once been a reasonable reaction to an outage, and each had been kept because deleting an alert feels riskier than ignoring one.

The pager went off about eleven times a week. Roughly nine of those pages were closed within a minute by whoever woke up, with no action taken. People stopped trusting the sound. That is the real cost: the tenth page, the one that mattered, arrived to someone who had already learned to roll over.

Count what wakes people

The first step needs no tooling. For one month, write down every page with three facts: what fired, what the person did, and how long it took. Do not judge yet. A spreadsheet is enough.

At the end of the month the pattern is usually blunt. A handful of rules cause most of the noise, and most of those rules describe a symptom that fixes itself.

The number of pages that needed action did not move. Everything else did. The median time went up because the remaining pages were real problems, which take longer than a reflex dismissal.

If an alert has never led to a human decision, it is not an alert. It is a log line with a loud voice.

Rules that survive the cut

Keep a rule only if it passes all three tests:

  1. A person can do something useful within the first ten minutes.
  2. Waiting until morning would cost money, data or trust.
  3. The alert text names the next step, not just the symptom.

Everything else becomes a daily digest, a dashboard tile, or nothing. A rule that fails the first test can still be valuable as a ticket; it should simply not ring a phone.

# before: fires on any spike, at any hour
alert: queue_depth_high
  when: queue_depth > 1000
  notify: pager

# after: fires only when it has been true long enough to matter
alert: queue_not_draining
  when: queue_depth > 1000 for 15m and drain_rate < 1
  notify: pager
  note: "Check the consumer. Restart is safe. Runbook: queue-drain."

The second rule is longer and looks fussier. It also says what is wrong, how long it has been wrong, and what to try first. Press Ctrl + F through your own rules for the word notify: pager and read each one aloud.

Questions people ask

Will we miss a real incident?

Less often than before. A page that is read carefully beats ten that are skimmed. Keep a weekly review of anything that was demoted, and promote it back if it earns the place.

Who owns the cleanup?

Whoever carries the pager that week. Ownership works better when the person who feels the noise can also delete it.
